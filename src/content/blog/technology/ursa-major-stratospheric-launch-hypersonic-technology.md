<h3>The Stratospheric Advantage</h3>

<p>
Launching hypersonic vehicles from stratospheric altitude offers significant advantages over ground-based alternatives. The thin atmosphere at high altitude reduces drag during initial acceleration, allowing vehicles to reach hypersonic speeds more efficiently. Stratolaunch's massive carrier aircraft, the world's largest plane by wingspan, provides an airborne launch platform that eliminates the need for extensive ground infrastructure.
</p>

<p>
Ursa Major's partnership with Stratolaunch creates a synergistic relationship between launch platform and propulsion system. The Talon-A reusable hypersonic vehicle mounts beneath the carrier aircraft's wing, then releases at altitude to fire its rocket engine. This architecture enables rapid turnaround between flights, making frequent testing economically feasible for the first time.
</p>

<h2>Inside the Draper Engine: Propulsion Reinvented</h2>

<p>
At the heart of Ursa Major's hypersonic capabilities lies the Draper engine, a liquid-fueled propulsion system that represents a fundamental departure from conventional rocket design. Developed in collaboration with the US Air Force Research Laboratory, this engine addresses longstanding limitations that have constrained hypersonic vehicle performance.
</p>

<p>
Traditional approaches to hypersonic propulsion have faced difficult trade-offs. Glide vehicles launched from high altitude rely entirely on gravity and aerodynamics, sacrificing control and maneuverability. Solid rocket boosters provide thrust but cannot be throttled or shut down once ignited. Liquid-fueled alternatives often depend on cryogenic propellants requiring complex handling procedures or toxic chemicals that create safety and logistical challenges.
</p>

<p>
The Draper engine breaks through these constraints with an innovative propellant combination and clever engineering. It runs on high-test hydrogen peroxide and kerosene, fuels that are non-cryogenic, stable at room temperature, and relatively non-toxic. These characteristics enable safer storage, simpler handling, and reduced operational complexity compared to alternatives.
</p>

<h3>Catalytic Combustion Innovation</h3>

<p>
The genius of the Draper engine lies in its catalytic decomposition system. When hydrogen peroxide flows through the engine, it encounters a catalyst that decomposes the fuel into high-pressure oxygen and steam. This reaction serves dual purposes. The steam drives the engine's turbopumps, while the oxygen combusts with kerosene in a mixture that ignites spontaneously.
</p>

<p>
This design eliminates complex ignition systems and enables capabilities impossible with conventional rockets. The engine can be throttled during flight, adjusting thrust as mission requirements demand. It can even be shut down and restarted, providing flexibility for complex flight profiles. These characteristics transform what hypersonic vehicles can accomplish, enabling sustained powered flight rather than brief boosts followed by unpowered glides.
</p>

<h2>HAVOC Missile: Affordable Hypersonic Warfare</h2>

<p>
The HAVOC missile system embodies Ursa Major's commitment to practical, producible hypersonic weapons. This medium-range vehicle achieves speeds exceeding Mach 5 while addressing the cost and manufacturing challenges that have limited hypersonic system deployment. The design philosophy prioritizes attributes that matter for operational military systems: reliability, affordability, and scalability.
</p>

<p>
Traditional hypersonic programs have produced exquisite systems in small numbers, treating each vehicle as a precision instrument requiring extensive hand-crafting. This approach creates capabilities that cannot be sustained in conflict, where losses occur and replacements must be available quickly. HAVOC represents a different paradigm, designed from inception for rapid production at volumes that operational scenarios require.
</p>

<p>
The missile achieves this manufacturability through extensive use of additive manufacturing. More than 80% of engine components are produced via 3D printing, dramatically reducing part count and simplifying assembly. Fewer parts mean fewer failure modes, faster production, and lower costs. The design also eliminates expensive thermal protection systems, further reducing both cost and complexity.
</p>

<h3>Modular Launch Architecture</h3>

<p>
Versatility characterizes the HAVOC system's launch options. Its modular design and compatibility with solid rocket boosters enable deployment from multiple platforms. Fighter aircraft, bombers, vertical launch systems, and ground-based launchers can all serve as launch points, adapting to mission requirements and available infrastructure.
</p>

<p>
This flexibility addresses a critical military need. Hypersonic weapons must integrate with existing force structures rather than requiring entirely new deployment systems. A missile that can launch from platforms already in service reduces implementation costs and accelerates operational availability. Commanders gain options for employing hypersonic capabilities across diverse scenarios.
</p>

<h2>Stratospheric Launch: The Talon-A Platform</h2>

<p>
Stratolaunch's Talon-A vehicle demonstrates the practical value of Ursa Major's propulsion technology in a reusable testing platform. This autonomous aircraft reaches speeds beyond Mach 5 while providing critical data about hypersonic flight characteristics. The Pentagon has recognized its value, contracting Stratolaunch to provide testing infrastructure for military hypersonic programs.
</p>

<p>
The Talon-A architecture solves fundamental problems that have constrained hypersonic testing. Traditional ground-based tests require expensive facilities and provide limited flight duration. Expendable test vehicles offer more realistic conditions but at prohibitive cost for extensive test programs. Talon-A bridges this gap, offering reusable high-speed flight at sustainable cost.
</p>

<p>
Mounted beneath Stratolaunch's carrier aircraft, Talon-A releases at altitude and fires its Ursa Major engine to accelerate into the hypersonic regime. After completing its test mission, the vehicle returns for recovery and refurbishment. This reusability enables iterative testing that rapidly advances technology maturity.
</p>

<h3>The Hadley Engine Evolution</h3>

<p>
Ursa Major's Hadley engine powers Talon-A missions with impressive capabilities. Producing 5,000 pounds of thrust, it operates on liquid oxygen and kerosene using an oxygen-rich staged combustion cycle. This design approach, more commonly found in large orbital-class engines, provides efficiency advantages that extend flight duration and performance.
</p>

<p>
The upgraded H13 variant, developed under the Pentagon contract, increases engine reusability with additional restart capability. Advanced materials enable more than twice as many missions compared to earlier versions, driving down cost per flight while supporting expanded test objectives. These improvements reflect Ursa Major's commitment to continuous enhancement rather than resting on initial achievements.
</p>

<h2>3D Printing and Manufacturing Innovation</h2>

<p>
Ursa Major's manufacturing approach exemplifies how additive technology transforms aerospace production. Traditional rocket engines require machining, welding, and assembly of hundreds of individual components. Each interface introduces potential failure points and requires inspection and quality verification. The result is expensive, time-consuming production of complex systems.
</p>

<p>
3D printing consolidates these multiple parts into integrated structures produced in single operations. Components that previously required assembly are printed as unified pieces, eliminating joints and reducing failure modes. The approach enables geometries impossible with conventional manufacturing, optimizing performance through designs that machining constraints would prohibit.
</p>

<p>
For hypersonic applications, these manufacturing advantages translate directly into operational benefits. Lower production costs enable larger inventories. Simplified designs improve reliability. Faster manufacturing responds more quickly to changing requirements. The combination addresses historical barriers that limited hypersonic system deployment.
</p>

<h2>Engineering Challenges Remaining</h2>

<p>
Despite impressive progress, significant engineering challenges remain before hypersonic systems achieve their full potential. Sustained flight at extreme speeds creates thermal, structural, and control problems that push materials and designs to their limits. Ursa Major's current solutions address propulsion, but integrated systems must solve multiple challenges simultaneously.
</p>

<p>
Thermal management stands among the most demanding issues. Air friction at hypersonic speeds generates temperatures that can destroy conventional materials. Advanced ceramics, refractory metals, and active cooling systems each offer partial solutions, but combining them into practical vehicles requires continued innovation. The HAVOC missile's elimination of expensive thermal protection suggests creative approaches to this challenge, but more work remains.</p>

<p>
Control and guidance at hypersonic speeds present additional difficulties. The same atmospheric density that enables aerodynamic maneuvering also creates heating and drag. Vehicles must navigate this trade-off while maintaining precision sufficient for their missions. Testing programs using Ursa Major's propulsion systems generate data that informs solutions to these control challenges.
